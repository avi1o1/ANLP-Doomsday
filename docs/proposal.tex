\begin{abstract}
On sparse-autoencoder features, BM25 scores $0.582$ nDCG@10 where a plain inner
product over the same vectors scores $0.381$. On SPLADE the ordering reverses,
$0.455$ against $0.568$. What BM25 adds is collection frequency, the rate at which
a feature fires across a corpus, and the published account of the reversal is that
this helps only while a vocabulary stays Zipfian. That account has been observed
one vocabulary at a time and never made predictive, so for a new sparse
representation the only way to pick a scorer is to run both. We make that choice
predictable. Eight vocabularies built from one frozen encoder are scored with a
BM25-style function whose four components toggle independently, and we regress the
gap between its corrected and uncorrected forms on six distributional measurements
that cost one forward pass, with one row per vocabulary and collection, since a
vocabulary has no single frequency curve. Block-sparse attention and mixture-of-experts routing pose
the same choice and consult no such statistic either, so the fitted predictor goes
to them without refitting, in units that carry across the three settings. Whether
it holds there is an open question. Attention sinks fire for almost every query yet
cannot be removed, and the rarest experts are the most critical, so frequency may
signal importance differently inside a model than across a corpus. Evaluation
covers fourteen collections, four of them Indic or code-mixed, where the
distributions the correction depends on are known to shift.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and Research Gap}
\label{sec:motivation}

A learned sparse retriever scores a query-document pair as
$\langle u_q, u_d \rangle$ over a concept vocabulary. A block-sparse attention
kernel estimates how critical KV page $i$ is for query $q_t$ as
$r_{t,i} = \langle q_t, c_i \rangle$ over page summaries, then attends to the
top-$k$ pages only \citep{tang2024quest}. A mixture-of-experts router gates on
$g_e = \langle h_t, w_e \rangle$ and sends the token to its top-$k$ experts
\citep{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch}. These belong to three different literatures
and perform one computation, an inner product against a learned non-negative basis
followed by a hard top-$k$.

What all three leave out is any account of how often a given feature wins.
Classical retrieval settled that question decades ago, and the correction has three
parts, namely inverse document frequency, which discounts features that fire
everywhere, term-frequency saturation, so that repeated evidence stops accumulating
linearly, and length normalisation \citep{robertson2009bm25}. Parts of it have been
reinvented inside the transformer under other names. Loss-free load balancing adds
a per-expert bias driven by recent load \citep{wang2024lossfree}, an additive
frequency discount applied during training with no saturation or length term, and
the auxiliary losses it replaces push toward uniform expert use
\citep{zoph2022stmoe}. Streaming inference hand-codes an exception for the
highest-frequency keys in the cache \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. Each of these is
a local fix.

The shortcoming in the published work is narrower than those local fixes suggest.
Two papers have run the comparison, both in retrieval, and both explain their result
the same way, that BM25 helps while a vocabulary keeps its Zipfian structure
\citep{clavie2026latent,han2026bm25v}. Neither varies the vocabulary
systematically, so the explanation rests on one vocabulary at a time. It has never
been turned into a quantity computable before the experiment, and never checked
where the same computation runs inside a model.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled family of sixteen representations from one frozen encoder, so
basis construction and granularity vary while representation learning stays fixed.
The published comparisons vary one vocabulary at a time.
\item One scoring function with four independent switches, so the plain inner
product and BM25 are two configurations of the same function and any gain names a
single mechanism.
\item A predictor in place of an explanation. Six diagnostics are regressed on the
gap between those two configurations over $224$ rows and validated on held-out basis
families and held-out collections, which turns the Zipfian account into something
usable in advance.
\item A test outside retrieval, which no single-setting study can run. The fitted
predictor is applied without refitting to KV-page selection and expert routing, in
units that carry across the three settings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

BM25 scores a document $d$ against query $Q$ as
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i \in Q} \idf_i \cdot
\frac{f_i(d)\,(k_1+1)}{f_i(d) + k_1\!\left(1 - b + b\frac{|d|}{\overline{dl}}\right)},
\label{eq:bm25}
\end{equation}
with $\idf_i = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{N - df_i + 0.5}{df_i + 0.5}\right)$. None of
these ingredients requires words. Each needs only a representation with
non-negative coordinates, a meaningful per-dimension collection frequency $df_i$,
and a magnitude that admits a notion of length. Dense embeddings have none of
these, since their coordinates are signed and every dimension is active in every
document, which is why dense retrieval is scored with a plain inner product.

Learned sparse features change the picture. Sparse-autoencoder activations,
$k$-means codes and quantised codebooks are non-negative, sparse and count-like.
KV page summaries are built from signed keys and so are not non-negative
themselves, but the quantity IDF needs is a selection count, which is non-negative
for pages and experts alike and well defined over a calibration set. All
three mechanisms are eligible for the same correction, so whether it helps is an
empirical question.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learned sparse retrieval and its vocabularies.} SPLADE and its
successors learn term weights over the backbone subword vocabulary and retain the
inverted index \citep{formal2021splade,lassance2024spladev3}. Recent work replaces
that vocabulary with a learned one. CL-SR indexes SAE-decomposed dense embeddings
as concept units \citep{park2025clsr}, SPLARE trains an SAE-based sparse retriever
directly \citep{formal2026splare}, and SAE-SPLADE swaps SPLADE's MLM head for an
SAE encoder \citep{zong2026tokens}. All three score with an inner product.

\paragraph{Scoring learned sparse features with BM25.}
\citet{clavie2026latent} show that SAE features taken from a frozen dense retriever
have roughly Zipfian collection statistics and are directly BM25-scorable. Their
Appendix~C holds the sharpest evidence for our question. On one suite BM25 reaches
$0.582$ nDCG@10 against $0.381$ for the inner product on SAE latent terms, and
$0.455$ against $0.568$ on SPLADE-v3, so the two scorers swap places once the
vocabulary changes beneath them. \citet{han2026bm25v} run the comparison on image
features and find (their \S4.4) that the two differ by at most about two points of
R@1, attributing the parity to top-$k$ filtering already suppressing what IDF would
down-weight. The same section reports that raising the per-patch activation budget
from $k{=}16$ to $k{=}128$ makes document frequencies converge and drops R@1 from
$0.508$ to $0.038$, so the effect is distributional. Both papers converge on the Zipfian
explanation of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, which is in print already and not ours
to supply.

\paragraph{Selection inside the transformer.} Quest scores KV pages by an inner
product against page min-max key summaries and loads only the top-$k$
\citep{tang2024quest}. H2O keeps a mix of recent and heavy-hitter tokens
\citep{zhang2023h2o}, and StreamingLLM preserves the earliest keys as attention
sinks \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. Expected Attention ranks a cached pair by how
strongly future queries are predicted to attend to it and prunes the low scorers
\citep{devoto2025expectedattention}. None of these uses the rate at which a page is
selected across queries. On the routing side, \citet{su2026superexperts} show that
a handful of rare experts are disproportionately critical. To our knowledge no work
treats retrieval scoring, KV selection and routing as one scoring problem.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{A modular scorer.} For every setting we implement one scoring function
in which soft IDF weighting, term-frequency saturation, length normalisation and
top-$k$ sparsification are separate switches. The uncorrected inner product is the
all-off configuration and BM25 is the all-on configuration. The quantity we predict
throughout is the margin,
\begin{equation}
m = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{on}} - Q_{\mathrm{off}}}{s(S,c)},
\end{equation}
the change in quality between two configurations at matched budget, divided by the
standard deviation $s(S,c)$ of that quality across the fixed set of switch
configurations in setting $S$ on collection $c$. The margin is then dimensionless
and means the same thing in every setting, which is what lets a model fitted in one
be scored in another. The primary metric is nDCG@10 in Setting~A, RULER accuracy in
Setting~B and MMLU accuracy in Setting~C, with perplexity entering as its negative
so that every metric points the same way.

\paragraph{Setting A: retrieval, used for calibration.} We build eight concept bases
from a single frozen encoder, spanning near-dense to strongly lexical, namely a
split-rectified identity map, random projection, PCA, $k$-means codes, product
quantisation \citep{jegou2011pq}, residual quantisation, a Top-$K$ sparse
autoencoder \citep{gao2025topksae}, and SPLADE
\citep{formal2021splade,lassance2024spladev3}. SPLADE is in the set on purpose,
since the reversal we explain is SPLADE against SAE. Each basis is instantiated at
pooled and token-level granularity, giving sixteen representations, with granularity
as an explicit variable.

\paragraph{Setting B: KV-page selection.} We start from Quest-style page criticality
\citep{tang2024quest} and add an online statistic $\sfreq_i$, the fraction of
prefill queries for which page $i$ enters the top-$k$. The corrected score
multiplies the saturated criticality by $\idf(\sfreq_i)$ and by a length term in
$\|c_i\|$, which is the scorer of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bm25} with pages for documents. The
method needs no training and drops into an existing kernel. Whether attention sinks
are exempted from the correction is an ablation rather than an assumption. If IDF
suppresses them and accuracy falls, that outcome is the mechanism result.

\paragraph{Setting C: expert routing.} The same correction applies to router logits
at inference time on OLMoE-1B-7B \citep{muennighoff2025olmoe}, which activates 8 of
64 experts per layer, with expert selection frequency measured over calibration
data as in Setting~B. Loss-free balancing
\citep{wang2024lossfree} is the additive, training-time special case we compare
against, and we check whether the correction protects or destroys the Super Experts
of \citet{su2026superexperts}.

\paragraph{Diagnostics and the predictor.} From a single forward pass over
calibration data we compute six measurements, namely active fraction per item, the
Zipf exponent of the selection-frequency curve, the Gini coefficient of activation
mass, head mass, the variance of IDF values, and sink share, meaning the fraction
of score mass held by the top $1\%$ most-frequently-selected features.
\citet{han2026bm25v} report several descriptively, with power-law
exponents in $[1.20, 2.32]$, but predict nothing
with them. One row of the regression pairs a representation with a collection,
because collection frequency belongs to a corpus and a basis therefore has no single
frequency curve. Measuring it again on
each collection is free, since indexing already performs that pass. Sixteen
representations across the fourteen collections of Section~\ref{sec:data} give
$224$ rows for six regressors.

\paragraph{Theory.} We aim to characterise when an IDF-weighted linear scorer over a
learned basis provably reduces to a bilinear inner product on the underlying dense
embeddings, which we call the collapse condition. Where it holds the two scorers
must agree, which explains part of the grid instead of tabulating it.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

Setting~A runs over fourteen collections. Bases are fitted and hyperparameters
tuned on MS~MARCO passage \citep{bajaj2016msmarco}, which supplies $8.8$M passages,
and tuning happens nowhere else, so every other collection tests generalisation.
LIMIT \citep{weller2025limit} separates lexical from
semantic signal by construction and should make any BM25 advantage easy to see.
TREC-DL 19 and 20 and six BEIR datasets \citep{thakur2021beir}, namely NFCorpus,
SciFact, FiQA-2018, TREC-COVID, ArguAna and SCIDOCS, cover the domains over which
the published reversal was reported.

Because collection frequency is a property of a language and a corpus rather than
of a model, the remaining four collections are the Hindi, Bengali and Telugu
portions of MIRACL \citep{zhang2023miracl} and a set of Hindi-English code-mixed
queries, where transliteration and mixing distort the document-frequency curve.
Neither prior SAE-based sparse retriever touches an Indic language, and the
multilingual evaluation in \citet{zong2026tokens} covers six MIRACL languages of
which none is Indian. Corpus-level measures of mixing already predict downstream
behaviour there \citep{kodali2022symcom}. Because every collection contributes its
own rows, these four enter the fit itself, and they add under $2$M documents.

Setting~B uses RULER \citep{hsieh2024ruler}, needle-in-a-haystack and LongBench on
Llama-3.2-1B/3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Setting~C uses OLMoE-1B-7B with
WikiText for perplexity and MMLU and GSM8K for downstream accuracy.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:eval}

For Setting~A we report nDCG@10, MRR@10 and Recall@100/1000 alongside index size,
FLOPs and latency, since a gain that destroys the efficiency of sparsity is worth
less. As a pipeline check we reproduce the Appendix~C reversal of
\citet{clavie2026latent} within a stated tolerance before trusting our own numbers.
Setting~B reports accuracy at matched token budget against Quest and H2O
\citep{tang2024quest,zhang2023h2o}, and Setting~C reports MMLU, GSM8K, perplexity
and routing entropy.

The headline result is the diagnostics-to-margin regression. The fitted model emits
three quantities we can score, the sign of the margin, measured by accuracy and
AUC, its magnitude in units of $s$, measured by held-out $R^2$ within retrieval and
by mean absolute error when the retrieval-fitted model is carried to Settings~B
and~C without refitting, and the ordering of configurations within a setting,
measured by Kendall's $\tau$. Rows are nested by representation and by collection,
so we cluster standard errors on both axes and validate by holding out whole groups
on each, four basis families and fourteen collections, reported separately, since
generalising to an unseen construction method and generalising to an unseen corpus
are different claims. Much of the raw variance sits between collections, so the
number we report is the gain in held-out $R^2$ over a baseline carrying collection
indicators alone. No model wins that comparison by learning which corpus is easy.

The analysis is fixed in advance, since the grid produces several hundred pairwise
comparisons. The primary comparison is all-on against all-off per representation
and component ablations are secondary. Significance uses a paired bootstrap over
queries with Holm--Bonferroni correction within each family of comparisons, effect
sizes are reported alongside $p$-values, and rows are weighted by the inverse
variance of the margin estimate, since a small code-mixed query set gives a noisier
margin than MS~MARCO dev. The transfer holds if the predicted sign is correct on at
least $70\%$ of held-out rows in Settings~B and~C and the mean absolute error there
is no worse than $1.5$ times the residual spread inside retrieval.

\section{Feasibility}
\label{sec:feasibility}

Nine weeks separate this proposal from the final submission. The retrieval grid over
all fourteen collections runs first and Setting~B follows it, so a transformer
result exists by the mid submission, leaving the predictor, the cross-setting
transfer and the ablations for the weeks after that. The four of us split the work
by setting, with the scorer and the diagnostics shared.

The hardware available to us provides nodes of four GPUs with $11$\,GB of device
memory each, which shapes the design. MS~MARCO is encoded once with a frozen
DistilBERT-scale encoder and every pooled basis is a linear or quantisation map
over the cached embeddings, with token-level granularity capped at a $1$M-passage
subsample. Settings~B and~C are inference-only, and we chose OLMoE-1B-7B over a
checkpoint such as Qwen3-30B-A3B because its $6.9$B parameters fit a node at
$4$-bit where the $30$B model would not, while still exposing 64 experts per layer.

\section{Risks and Limitations}
\label{sec:risks}

Confirming that BM25 helps on Zipfian vocabularies would only repeat what the cited
papers predict, so the informative outcomes are the awkward ones. A predicted gain where
the measured effect is negative would show contribution to the output and
discrimination between queries coming apart inside the transformer. Diagnostics promising a gain that never appears would falsify the
Zipfian account as stated. If the predictor is weak on both held-out axes we report
the grid as a benchmark and the collapse condition as the theoretical contribution,
a negative result with a resource attached. If Setting~C proves infeasible
we fall back to Settings~A and~B, which still support the transfer claim.

Three limitations stand out. Setting~A uses a single frozen encoder, so basis and
encoder effects are not fully separable. Setting~C is inference-time only, so we
claim nothing about training dynamics. The diagnostics are correlational, and only
the transfer test is causal.
