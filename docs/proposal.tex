\begin{abstract}
Retrieval, long-context attention and sparse mixture-of-experts all pick a few items
out of many, which documents to return, which cached tokens to attend to, which
experts to route through, by an inner product against learned features and a hard
top-$k$. Retrieval learned long ago to down-weight
an item that fires across the whole corpus, the collection-frequency term in BM25. On
sparse-autoencoder features BM25 reaches $0.582$ nDCG@10 where the inner product
reaches $0.381$, and on SPLADE the order reverses, $0.455$ against $0.568$. The
published account is that the correction helps while a vocabulary stays Zipfian, but it
has never been made predictive, so for a new representation the only way to choose a
scorer is to run both. We predict the choice in advance. From one frozen encoder we
build sixteen representations, eight bases at two granularities, and score each with a
single BM25-style function whose four components switch independently, so the inner
product and BM25 are two of its configurations. Across fourteen collections, four of
them Indic or code-mixed, we regress the gap between the two configurations on six
diagnostics that cost one forward pass, $224$ rows in all, and validate on held-out
basis families and corpora. Attention and routing pose the same choice and reinvent only
fragments of the correction, so we carry the fitted predictor to them unchanged and
score it on one effect-size scale. Whether it holds there is open, since frequency
and importance need not align inside a model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and Research Gap}
\label{sec:motivation}

A learned sparse retriever scores a query-document pair as
$\langle u_q, u_d \rangle$ over a concept vocabulary. A block-sparse attention
kernel estimates how critical KV page $i$ is for query $q_t$ as
$r_{t,i} = \langle q_t, c_i \rangle$ over page summaries, then attends to the
top-$k$ pages only \citep{tang2024quest}. A mixture-of-experts router gates on
$g_e = \langle h_t, w_e \rangle$ and sends the token to its top-$k$ experts
\citep{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch}. These belong to three different literatures
and perform one computation, an inner product against a learned non-negative basis
followed by a hard top-$k$.

None of the three accounts for how often a given feature is selected.
Classical retrieval does, with a correction in three
parts, namely inverse document frequency, which discounts features that fire
everywhere, term-frequency saturation, so that repeated evidence stops accumulating
linearly, and length normalisation \citep{robertson2009bm25}. Parts of it have been
reinvented inside the transformer under other names. Loss-free load balancing adds
a per-expert bias driven by recent load \citep{wang2024lossfree}, an additive
frequency discount applied during training with no saturation or length term, which
replaces an auxiliary loss that pushes toward uniform expert use \citep{zoph2022stmoe}.
Streaming inference hand-codes an exception for the
most-attended keys in the cache \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. Each is a local fix.

The shortcoming in the published work is narrower than those local fixes suggest.
Three papers have scored the same features both ways, all in retrieval, and the
correction wins twice \citep{clavie2026latent,park2025clsr} and comes out level once
\citep{han2026bm25v}. Clavi\'e et al. and Han et al. explain it the same way, that
BM25 helps while a vocabulary keeps its Zipfian structure. None of the three varies
the vocabulary systematically, so the account rests on a handful of vocabularies. No
one has turned it into a quantity that can be computed before the experiment, or
checked it where the same computation runs inside a model.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled family of sixteen representations from one frozen encoder, so
basis construction and granularity vary while representation learning stays fixed.
No published comparison varies them systematically.
\item One scoring function with four independent switches, so the plain inner
product and BM25 are two configurations of one function, and any gain can be traced
to a single mechanism.
\item A predictor for the sign and size of the gain. The gap between those two
configurations is regressed on six diagnostics over $224$ rows and validated on
held-out basis families and held-out corpora, which turns the Zipfian account into
something usable in advance.
\item A test outside retrieval, which no single-setting study can run. The fitted
predictor is applied without refitting to KV-page selection and expert routing, on
one effect-size scale across the three settings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

BM25 scores a document $d$ against query $Q$ as
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i \in Q} \idf_i \cdot
\frac{f_i(d)\,(k_1+1)}{f_i(d) + k_1\!\left(1 - b + b\frac{|d|}{\overline{dl}}\right)},
\label{eq:bm25}
\end{equation}
with $\idf_i = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{N - df_i + 0.5}{df_i + 0.5}\right)$, the smoothed
variant, which stays non-negative. None of
these ingredients requires words. Each needs only a representation with
non-negative coordinates, a meaningful per-dimension collection frequency $df_i$,
and a magnitude that admits a notion of length. Dense embeddings have none of
these, since their coordinates are signed and every dimension is active in every
document, which is why dense retrieval is scored with a plain inner product.

Learned sparse features change the picture. Sparse-autoencoder activations,
$k$-means codes and quantised codebooks are non-negative, sparse and count-like.
KV page summaries are built from signed keys, but the quantity IDF needs is a
selection count, which is non-negative for pages and experts alike and well defined
over a calibration set. All three mechanisms are eligible for the same correction, so
whether it helps is an empirical question.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learned sparse retrieval and its vocabularies.} SPLADE learns term weights
over the subword vocabulary and keeps the inverted index
\citep{formal2021splade,lassance2024spladev3}. Recent work replaces that vocabulary
with a learned one. CL-SR indexes SAE-decomposed dense embeddings
as concept units \citep{park2025clsr}, SPLARE trains an SAE-based sparse retriever
directly \citep{formal2026splare}, and SAE-SPLADE swaps SPLADE's MLM head for an
SAE encoder \citep{zong2026tokens}. SPLARE and SAE-SPLADE score by inner product. CL-SR
weights activations by IDF instead, and dropping that weighting costs it 11 to 18
percent of its nDCG@10 on TREC-DL, the one published ablation of the correction we
study.

\paragraph{Scoring learned sparse features with BM25.}
\citet{clavie2026latent} show that SAE features taken from a frozen dense retriever
have roughly Zipfian collection statistics and are directly BM25-scorable. Their
Appendix~C holds the sharpest evidence for our question. On one suite BM25 reaches
$0.582$ nDCG@10 against $0.381$ for the inner product on SAE latent terms, and
$0.455$ against $0.568$ on SPLADE-v3, so the two scorers swap places when the
vocabulary changes. \citet{han2026bm25v} run the comparison on image
features and find (their \S4.4) that the two differ by about two points of R@1 on
most of their benchmarks, attributing the parity to top-$k$ filtering already
suppressing what IDF would down-weight. The same section reports that raising the per-patch activation budget
from $k{=}16$ to $k{=}128$ makes document frequencies converge and drops R@1 from
$0.508$ to $0.038$, so the effect is distributional. Both papers give the Zipfian
explanation of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, which is already published. We take it
as given and turn it into a predictor.

\paragraph{Selection inside the transformer.} Quest scores KV pages by an inner
product against page min-max key summaries and loads only the top-$k$
\citep{tang2024quest}. H2O keeps a mix of recent and heavy-hitter tokens
\citep{zhang2023h2o}, and StreamingLLM preserves the earliest keys as attention
sinks \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. Expected Attention ranks a cached pair by how
strongly future queries are predicted to attend to it and prunes the low scorers
\citep{devoto2025expectedattention}. None of these uses the rate at which an entry is
selected across queries. On the routing side, \citet{su2026superexperts} identify a
handful of experts by activation magnitude, a quantity that selection rate does not
track, and pruning three of $6{,}144$ degrades the model badly. To our knowledge no work treats retrieval
scoring, KV selection and routing as one scoring problem.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{A modular scorer.} For every setting we implement one scoring function
in which soft IDF weighting, term-frequency saturation, length normalisation and
top-$k$ sparsification are separate switches. Top-$k$ off leaves the basis at its own
sparsity, and on it truncates each vector to its $k$ largest coordinates. The uncorrected
inner product is the all-off configuration and BM25 is the all-on configuration. The quantity we predict
throughout is the margin,
\begin{equation}
m = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{on}} - Q_{\mathrm{off}}}{s(S,c)},
\end{equation}
the change in quality between two configurations at matched budget, divided by the
standard deviation $s(S,c)$ of that quality across the fixed set of switch
configurations in setting $S$ on collection $c$. The margin is then dimensionless
and means the same thing in every setting, which lets a model fitted in one
be scored in another. The primary metric is nDCG@10 in Setting~A, RULER accuracy in
Setting~B and MMLU accuracy in Setting~C, and any perplexity enters as its negative,
so every metric points the same way.

\paragraph{Setting A: retrieval, used for calibration.} We build eight concept bases
from a single frozen encoder, spanning near-dense to strongly lexical, namely a
split-rectified identity map, random projection, PCA, $k$-means codes, product
quantisation \citep{jegou2011pq}, residual quantisation, a Top-$K$ sparse
autoencoder \citep{gao2025topksae}, and SPLADE
\citep{formal2021splade,lassance2024spladev3}. SPLADE is in the set on purpose,
since the reversal we explain is SPLADE against SAE. The eight fall into four families, linear,
clustered, quantised and learned, which are the groups the folds remove. Each basis
is instantiated at pooled and token-level granularity, giving sixteen
representations, with granularity as an explicit variable.

\paragraph{Setting B: KV-page selection.} We start from Quest-style page criticality
\citep{tang2024quest} and add an online statistic $\sfreq_i$, the fraction of
prefill queries for which page $i$ enters the top-$k$. The corrected score
multiplies the saturated criticality by $\idf(\sfreq_i)$ and by a length term in
$\|c_i\|$, which is the scorer of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bm25} with pages for documents. The
method needs no training and drops into an existing kernel. Whether attention sinks
are exempted is itself an ablation, and if IDF suppresses them and
accuracy falls, that is the mechanism result.

\paragraph{Setting C: expert routing.} The same correction applies to router logits
at inference time on OLMoE-1B-7B \citep{muennighoff2025olmoe}, which activates 8 of
64 experts per layer and whose $6.9$B parameters fit one node at $4$-bit, with expert
selection frequency measured over calibration data as in Setting~B. Loss-free
balancing \citep{wang2024lossfree} is the additive, training-time special case we
compare against. We also check whether the correction protects or destroys the Super
Experts of \citet{su2026superexperts}, which sets frequency against magnitude
directly.

\paragraph{Diagnostics and the predictor.} From a single forward pass over
calibration data we compute six measurements, namely active fraction per item, the
Zipf exponent of the selection-frequency curve, the Gini coefficient of activation
mass, head mass, the variance of IDF values, and sink share, meaning the fraction
of score mass held by the top $1\%$ most-frequently-selected features.
\citet{han2026bm25v} report several of these descriptively but predict nothing
with them. One row of the regression pairs a representation with a collection,
because collection frequency belongs to a corpus and a basis therefore has no single
frequency curve. Measuring it again on each collection is free, since indexing
already performs that pass. Sixteen representations across the fourteen collections
of Section~\ref{sec:data} give $224$ rows for six regressors.

\paragraph{Theory.} We aim to characterise when an IDF-weighted linear scorer over a
learned basis provably reduces to a bilinear inner product on the dense embeddings,
the collapse condition. Where it holds the two scorers must agree, which explains
part of the grid instead of tabulating it.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

Setting~A runs over fourteen collections drawn from twelve corpora. Bases are fitted
and hyperparameters tuned on MS~MARCO passage \citep{bajaj2016msmarco}, $8.8$M
passages, and nowhere else, so every other collection tests generalisation. LIMIT \citep{weller2025limit} is built from a capacity
argument, that the top-$k$ sets a query can return are bounded by embedding
dimension, and lexical scoring does well on it because its dimension is high, which
is the axis our bases are built to span. TREC-DL 19 and 20 and six BEIR datasets
\citep{thakur2021beir} cover the domains over which the published reversal was
reported. The two TREC-DL query sets run over the MS~MARCO documents, so those three
collections share one set of diagnostics.

Because collection frequency is a property of a language and a corpus rather than
of a model, the remaining four collections are the Hindi, Bengali and Telugu
portions of MIRACL \citep{zhang2023miracl} and a set of Hindi-English code-mixed
queries, where transliteration flattens the document-frequency curve.
SPLARE reports multilingual retrieval quality on Indic languages but measures no
frequency structure at all, and the multilingual evaluation in
\citet{zong2026tokens} covers six MIRACL languages of which none is Indian.
Corpus-level measures of code-mixing already tell such datasets apart where
token-level ones cannot \citep{kodali2022symcom}. Because every collection
contributes its own rows, these four enter the fit itself.

Setting~B uses RULER \citep{hsieh2024ruler}, needle-in-a-haystack and LongBench on
Llama-3.2-1B/3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Setting~C uses OLMoE-1B-7B with
WikiText for perplexity and MMLU and GSM8K for downstream accuracy.

\section{Evaluation Plan}
\label{sec:eval}

For Setting~A we report nDCG@10, MRR@10 and Recall@100/1000 alongside index size,
FLOPs and latency, since a gain that destroys the efficiency of sparsity is worth
less. As a pipeline check we reproduce the Appendix~C reversal of
\citet{clavie2026latent} within a stated tolerance before trusting our own numbers.
Setting~B reports accuracy at matched token budget against Quest and H2O
\citep{tang2024quest,zhang2023h2o}.

The headline result is the diagnostics-to-margin regression. The fitted model emits
three quantities we can score, the sign of the margin, measured by accuracy and
AUC, its magnitude in units of $s$, measured by held-out $R^2$ in retrieval and by
mean absolute error when it is carried to Settings~B and~C without refitting, and
the ordering of configurations within a setting, measured by Kendall's $\tau$. Rows are nested by representation and by corpus,
so we cluster standard errors on both axes and validate by holding out whole groups
on each, the four basis families and the twelve corpora, reported separately, since
generalising to an unseen construction method and generalising to an unseen corpus
are different claims. Grouping is by corpus rather than by collection, because query
sets over one document set share their diagnostics exactly. Since much of the raw
variance sits between corpora, the number we report is the gain in held-out $R^2$
over a baseline carrying corpus indicators alone, and no model wins that comparison
by learning which corpus is easy.

The analysis is fixed in advance, since the grid produces several hundred pairwise
comparisons. The primary comparison is all-on against all-off per representation
and component ablations are secondary. Significance uses a paired bootstrap over
queries with Holm--Bonferroni correction within each family of comparisons, effect
sizes are reported alongside $p$-values, and rows are weighted by the inverse
variance of the margin estimate, since a small code-mixed query set gives a noisier
margin than MS~MARCO dev. The transfer holds if the predicted sign is correct on at
least $70\%$ of held-out rows in Settings~B and~C and the mean absolute error there
is no worse than $1.5$ times the residual spread inside retrieval.

\section{Risks and Limitations}
\label{sec:risks}

The failures are the informative outcomes. Confirming that BM25 helps on
Zipfian vocabularies only repeats what the cited papers predict. A predicted gain where the
measured effect is negative would show contribution to the output and discrimination
between queries coming apart inside the transformer. Diagnostics promising a gain that never appears would falsify the
Zipfian account as stated. If the predictor is weak on both held-out axes we report
the grid as a benchmark and the collapse condition as the theoretical contribution. If
Setting~C proves infeasible we fall back to Settings~A and~B, which still support the
transfer claim.

Three limitations stand out. Setting~A uses a single frozen encoder, so basis and
encoder effects are not fully separable. Setting~C is inference-time only, so we claim
nothing about training dynamics. The diagnostics are correlational throughout, and
only the transfer test carries any causal weight.
