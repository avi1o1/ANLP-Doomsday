\begin{abstract}
Learned sparse retrieval, block-sparse attention and mixture-of-experts routing
all do the same thing. Each scores a query against a set of learned non-negative
features by inner product and keeps the top-$k$, and none of them uses how often
a feature fires across the collection, which is the statistic BM25 is built on.
Whether that omission costs anything is unsettled even in retrieval, where
replacing BM25 with a plain inner product over the same vocabulary drops nDCG@10
from $0.582$ to $0.381$ on sparse-autoencoder features and raises it from $0.455$
to $0.568$ on SPLADE. The published account is that BM25 helps while a vocabulary
stays Zipfian, but that has only been observed one vocabulary at a time and never
turned into a prediction. We build eight concept vocabularies from one frozen
encoder, score each with a BM25-style function whose components toggle
independently, and regress the resulting margin on six
cheap distributional measurements. The fitted predictor then goes, without refitting, to KV-page
selection and expert routing. Whether it holds there is open. Attention sinks
fire constantly yet cannot be removed, and the rarest experts in a
mixture-of-experts are the most critical, so frequency may signal importance
differently inside a model than across a corpus.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Same Computation in Three Places}

A learned sparse retriever scores a query-document pair as
$\langle u_q, u_d \rangle$ over a concept vocabulary. A block-sparse attention
kernel estimates how critical KV page $i$ is for query $q_t$ as
$r_{t,i} = \langle q_t, c_i \rangle$ over page summaries and attends to the
top-$k$ pages only \citep{tang2024quest}. A mixture-of-experts router gates on
$g_e = \langle h_t, w_e \rangle$ and sends each token to its top-$k$ experts.
These come from three literatures and perform one computation, an inner product
against a learned non-negative basis, then a hard top-$k$.

None of them asks how often a feature wins. BM25 has corrected exactly that for
decades, discounting features that fire everywhere through inverse document
frequency, saturating repeated evidence, and normalising for length
\citep{robertson2009bm25}. None of those ingredients needs words. Each needs
only a representation with non-negative coordinates, a meaningful per-dimension
collection frequency, and a sensible notion of magnitude, and learned sparse
features supply all three.
Fragments of the correction keep reappearing unrecognised. The per-expert load
bias of loss-free balancing is an additive frequency discount with no saturation
or length term \citep{wang2024lossfree}, and streaming inference hard-codes an
exception for the highest-frequency keys \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. The need
is felt case by case, but no general account says when the correction helps.

\section{What Is Already Known}

\citet{clavie2026latent} show that sparse-autoencoder features taken from a
frozen dense retriever have roughly Zipfian collection statistics and are
directly BM25-scorable. Their Appendix~C carries the sharpest evidence. On one
suite BM25 reaches $0.582$ nDCG@10 against $0.381$ for the inner product on SAE
latent terms, and $0.455$ against $0.568$ on SPLADE-v3, so the two scorers swap
places when only the vocabulary changes. \citet{han2026bm25v} run the same
comparison on image features and find the two within about two points of R@1
once top-$k$ filtering is applied, which they attribute to top-$k$ already
suppressing what IDF would down-weight. Two further papers build
SAE-based sparse retrievers and score them by inner product without ever running
the comparison \citep{formal2026splare,zong2026tokens}.

Both papers that did compare propose the same mechanism, that BM25 helps while a
vocabulary keeps its Zipfian structure. That explanation is already in print and
we are not claiming it. It has only been seen one vocabulary at a time, never
turned into a prediction, and never tested outside retrieval. CL-SR
\citep{park2025clsr}, the nearest published system, indexes SAE-decomposed
embeddings as concept units, whereas we leave representation learning alone and
study the scorer.

\section{Why the Transfer Might Fail}

Inside the transformer, how often a feature fires and how much it matters come
apart in both directions. Attention sinks are the most frequently attended keys
in a cache, which by IDF logic makes them the least informative, yet deleting
them collapses generation \citep{xiao2024streamingllm}. \citet{su2026superexperts}
report the mirror case, where removing three experts out of $6{,}144$ badly
degrades Qwen3-30B-A3B, so the rarest experts are the most critical. IDF predicts
that second case, but would also promote thousands of merely rare experts. A
recent KV-cache method ranks entries by expected future attention and prunes the
low scorers \citep{devoto2025expectedattention}, the opposite ordering to IDF,
and it works. Either the map from frequency to importance inverts between a
corpus and a model, or two quantities have been conflated, how much a feature
contributes to the output and how much it separates one query from another.
Distinguishing them is what the transfer experiment is for.

\section{Plan}

We hold representation learning fixed and vary the basis and the scorer
independently. Each setting gets one scoring function in which soft IDF,
saturation, length normalisation and top-$k$ sparsification are independent
switches, so the plain inner product is the all-off case and BM25 the all-on
case, and any gain is attributable to a mechanism rather than a bundle.

\paragraph{Bases and data.} Eight bases come from one frozen encoder and span
near-dense to strongly lexical, namely split-rectified identity, random
projection, PCA, $k$-means, product and residual quantisation, a Top-$K$ SAE,
and SPLADE \citep{formal2021splade}. SPLADE matters because the reversal we are
explaining is SPLADE against SAE. Running every basis at pooled and token-level granularity
gives sixteen representations, making granularity a variable rather than a
confound. We tune only on MS~MARCO, add LIMIT \citep{weller2025limit}
because it separates lexical from semantic signal by construction, and transfer
zero-shot to a BEIR subset and TREC-DL 19/20, reproducing the Appendix~C reversal
first. Encoding MS~MARCO once dominates the cost, since every basis is then a
linear map over cached embeddings.

\paragraph{Languages.} Collection frequency belongs to a corpus and a language
rather than to a model, so a predictor built on it should be tested where the
distribution moves. No prior SAE retriever covers an Indic language, and the
multilingual evaluation of \citet{zong2026tokens} spans six MIRACL languages,
none Indian. We repeat the grid on the Hindi, Bengali and Telugu
portions of MIRACL \citep{zhang2023miracl} and on Hindi-English code-mixed
queries, where transliteration and mixing flatten the document-frequency curve.
Corpus-level measures of mixing already predict downstream behaviour there
\citep{kodali2022symcom}, and our diagnostics are instruments of the same kind.

\paragraph{Predictor.} One forward pass per basis gives active fraction, the
Zipf exponent of the selection-frequency curve, Gini of activation mass, head
mass, IDF variance, and the score mass held by the top $1\%$ of features.
\citet{han2026bm25v} report several descriptively but never predict with them. We
regress the BM25-minus-inner-product margin on the six and validate by holding
out whole basis families, so the predictor is never scored on a construction
method it has seen.

\paragraph{Transfer.} The fitted predictor then goes unchanged to the two
transformer settings. For KV selection we take Quest page criticality
\citep{tang2024quest} and weight it by $\idf(\sfreq_i)$, where $\sfreq_i$ is the
fraction of prefill queries for which page $i$ enters the top-$k$; the saturation
and length switches carry over to the page score and its norm. The correction
needs no training, and whether sinks are exempted from it is an ablation rather
than an assumption. For routing we apply the same weighting to router logits at
inference on OLMoE-1B-7B \citep{muennighoff2025olmoe}, against loss-free
balancing as the training-time special case.

\paragraph{What would count as a result.} Confirming that BM25 helps on Zipfian
vocabularies restates what both comparison papers predict, so the useful outcomes
are the awkward ones. A predicted gain for KV selection where the measured effect
is negative would separate contribution to the output from discrimination between
queries. Diagnostics promising a gain where none appears would falsify the
Zipfian account.
