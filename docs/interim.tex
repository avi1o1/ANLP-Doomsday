\begin{abstract}
BM25-style scoring continues to outperform inner-product scoring on modern learned sparse representations, and several independent papers have each confirmed this on their own choice of vocabulary and scoring function. None of them, however, explains \emph{why}. Each fixes one sparse vocabulary and one scorer at a time, so the observed gain cannot be attributed to any specific statistical property of the representation, only to the particular pairing each paper happened to test. We address this by decoupling the two variables that prior work leaves confounded. We construct seven concept vocabularies from a single frozen encoder, spanning a range from near-dense to strongly lexical, and score every one of them with both a plain inner product and a modular BM25-style function whose components (soft IDF weighting, saturation, length normalisation, and top-$k$ sparsification) can be switched on or off independently. We then examine whether cheap, pre-retrieval diagnostics of each vocabulary's distribution can predict the resulting performance gap. The intended outcome is a practical, checkable rule for when BM25-style scoring is worth using over a learned sparse representation, along with a clearer picture of which of its mechanisms actually drive the gain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background and the Gap in Prior Work}
BM25 has survived decades of neural retrieval because its ingredients (rare terms are weighted more heavily, repeated evidence saturates instead of accumulating linearly, and long documents are discounted) are not actually tied to words. They only require a representation whose coordinates are non-negative, whose per-dimension collection frequency is meaningful, and whose overall magnitude has a sensible notion of ``document length.'' Ordinary dense embeddings satisfy none of this: coordinates are signed, every dimension is active in every document, and there is no natural length analogue. That is why dense retrieval has conventionally been scored with a plain inner product or cosine similarity instead.

Learned sparse representations, such as $k$-means codes, sparse autoencoder (SAE) activations, and quantised codebooks, change this picture, because they produce non-negative, sparse, count-like features of the kind BM25 was designed for. Four independent papers have already reported that BM25-style scoring outperforms inner-product scoring on one particular learned vocabulary \citep{clavie2026latent,han2026bm25v,formal2026splare,zong2026tokens}, and in at least one case the same pair of scorers reverses ranking depending on which vocabulary is used. None of these papers establishes \emph{why}: each fixes one construction method and one scorer and moves on, so it is impossible to tell whether the advantage comes from something fundamental about sparse, non-negative codes, or from an accident of the particular vocabulary each paper happened to build. This is the gap our project closes.

\section{Our Proposal}
We turn this gap into an experiment we can run directly. Rather than proposing yet another sparse encoder, we hold the representation-learning question fixed and instead vary, independently and systematically, (i) how the concept vocabulary is built and (ii) how it is scored. This lets any BM25-versus-inner-product gap be attributed to a property of the vocabulary rather than to an idiosyncrasy of a single method. Concretely, we build seven concept bases from a single frozen text encoder, spanning a range from ``dense-like'' to ``lexical-like'': a split-rectified identity mapping, random projections, PCA, $k$-means codes, product and residual vector quantisation, and a Top-$K$ sparse autoencoder. Each basis is instantiated at both a pooled (single vector per document) and a token-level (per-position) granularity, so granularity itself becomes a variable we test rather than a confound left unexamined, as it has been in prior work.

Every one of these fourteen representations is then scored two ways: a plain inner product, and a modular BM25-style scorer in which soft IDF weighting, term-frequency saturation, document-length normalisation, and top-$k$ sparsification can each be switched on or off independently. This modularity is the central methodological move: instead of asking only ``does BM25 beat the dot product,'' we can ask which specific ingredient drives the gain, and for which kind of vocabulary.

The second part of our proposal is predictive rather than purely comparative. Before running any retrieval, we compute cheap distributional diagnostics for each concept basis: how many dimensions are ``alive'' per document, how skewed the term-frequency distribution is (Zipf slope, Gini coefficient, head mass), and how much IDF values vary across the vocabulary. If informative, we can regress the observed BM25-minus-inner-product margin on them to get a usable rule of thumb, e.g. ``if a vocabulary looks like X, expect BM25 to help by roughly this much,'' without exhaustively benchmarking every new representation.

\section{What We Will Do}
The experimental grid is run primarily on MS MARCO passage retrieval and on LIMIT \citep{weller2026limit}, chosen deliberately because it creates a high-contrast setting between lexical and semantic retrieval signals and should make any BM25 advantage (or lack of one) easy to see. Once we have identified which configurations look interesting, we transfer them zero-shot to a subset of BEIR and to TREC-DL 19/20, tuning hyperparameters only on MS MARCO so that the transfer results are a genuine test of generalisation rather than of additional fitting. We report standard retrieval quality metrics (nDCG@10, MRR@10, Recall@100/1000) alongside efficiency numbers, namely index size, query-document FLOPs, and latency, since a scoring function that helps quality but destroys the efficiency benefits of sparsity would be a much less useful finding.

On the analysis side, the central result we are chasing is the diagnostics-to-margin regression described above, validated by holding out entire basis \emph{families} (for example, training the predictor on everything except quantisation-based methods and checking it still predicts the quantisation results reasonably). Alongside this we run ablations that switch individual BM25 components on and off to see which ones are actually necessary for which representations. As a smaller theoretical thread, we also aim to characterise the conditions under which an IDF-weighted BM25-style linear scorer provably collapses to an ordinary bilinear inner product on the underlying dense embeddings, which would explain, rather than just observe, when the two scoring functions should agree. As a sanity check on our own pipeline, we will attempt to reproduce a prior published result \citep{clavie2026latent} within a small tolerance before trusting our own new numbers.

\section{Why It Matters}
If this works, the payoff is not another retrieval model but a piece of understanding that is currently missing from the literature: a quantitative, checkable rule for predicting when BM25-style scoring is worth using over a learned sparse vocabulary, an account of which BM25 mechanisms matter under which distributional conditions, and a reusable benchmark and diagnostic toolkit that anyone building a new sparse concept vocabulary could run before committing to a scorer, rather than, as is currently the case, discovering the answer by trial and error after the fact.
